02 / Read the message

An email can look familiar and still be wrong.

Treat unexpected requests as unverified, even when the name, logo, or writing style seems right. These are useful signals, not a checklist you must complete.

Warning signs worth a second look
SENDER

Address mismatch

Display name looks familiar, but the full address or reply-to domain does not.

REQUEST

Unusual urgency

“Pay now,” “keep this secret,” or “your access ends today” discourages checking.

CONTENT

Unexpected attachment

A surprise invoice, shared document, QR code, or request for a password or MFA code.

A message to inspect
Training example · fictional

Payroll update required

From: Payroll Team <payroll@northstar-benefits.example>

“Your direct deposit will be paused. Confirm your details before 5 PM using the button below.”

What to notice
  • Did you expect a payroll change?
  • Does the domain match the employer's real domain?
  • Is a deadline pressuring you to skip a check?
  • Can you verify in the payroll app or with HR using a known contact?

The example uses the reserved .example domain; it is not a real login address.

03 / Before you sign in

A convincing login page proves very little.

Brand colors, familiar layouts, and even a padlock can appear on a deceptive page. The address and how you arrived there matter more.

Inspect the address, not the artwork
https://accounts.example.com.signin-check.attacker.example/verify

Fictional example: the controlling domain is attacker.example, not accounts.example.com. Read the hostname from right to left, stopping before the first single slash.

Stop if you see
  • A link from an unexpected email, text, or QR code
  • A domain with added words, misspellings, or an odd ending
  • A page asking for a password, recovery code, or MFA approval you did not start
  • A browser warning or a request to install a “security update”
Do this instead
  • Close the page; don't enter test credentials
  • Open the service from its official app or a bookmark
  • Check your password manager: it should not autofill on an unfamiliar domain
  • Deny unexpected MFA prompts; report them to IT or the service provider

04 / Check the destination

A polished website is not proof of legitimacy.

Fraudulent shopping, delivery, charity, and support sites often borrow familiar design cues. Slow down at the moment money, personal details, or a download is requested.

Signals to weigh together
01

Lookalike domain

Extra words, swapped characters, or an unusual top-level domain. A lock icon is not a trust certificate.

02

Hard-to-reach business

No verifiable address or independent contact details; support exists only in the suspicious message.

03

Odd payment route

Gift cards, cryptocurrency, wire transfers, or payment to a personal account for an ordinary purchase.

04

Too-good offer

Extreme discounts, fake countdowns, copied reviews, or a demand to pay before you can verify.

A safer check

Navigate to the store or service independently. Search for contact and refund details on the genuine site, and look for reviews from more than one independent source. For a charity appeal, visit the charity's known website directly. Don't use contact links or phone numbers supplied only by the suspicious page.

05 / The human angle

Social engineering aims at your next decision.

Phishing messages are designed to make a risky action feel routine. Recognizing the emotional lever gives you time to choose a safer route.

01

Urgency and fear

“Your account is locked.” “Pay by noon.” A short deadline makes verification feel costly.

02

Authority and impersonation

A message borrows the voice of a manager, bank, government office, or IT support.

03

Curiosity and reward

A package, shared file, unexpected refund, or exclusive offer tempts you to open first.

04

Familiarity and trust

Personal details from public profiles, breached data, or a compromised mailbox make a lie feel specific.

05

Scarcity and reciprocity

A limited offer or an apparent favor nudges you toward a quick “yes.”

06

Multi-channel pressure

An email is followed by a call or text that repeats the story and discourages asking anyone else.

Give yourself a verification pause

Stop before sending money, credentials, gift cards, documents, or one-time codes. Contact the person or organization using details you already trust, and ask a colleague when the request involves work or payment.

06 / Case file

A familiar supplier became a costly impersonation.

A documented business-email-compromise case shows how vendor trust and realistic payment requests can be exploited. It is related to phishing through social engineering, though the fraud centered on impersonation and invoices rather than a fake login page.

2013–2015

Google and Facebook lost more than $100 million to a fraud scheme involving fake invoices and email messages impersonating a hardware vendor. The U.S. Department of Justice described the scheme and announced the defendant's sentencing in 2019.

The lesson

Familiar business details can be copied.

A plausible invoice, known vendor name, or routine payment schedule is not independent proof that bank details or instructions are genuine.

The control

Verify changes out of band.

Confirm new payment details using a previously verified phone number or established vendor portal. Follow dual-approval rules and report unexpected changes.

07 / Make the safe move

One calm pause can change the outcome.

Use this short response routine for suspicious messages, pages, calls, and payment requests.

01 / PAUSEDon't interact

No links, attachments, replies, QR scans, codes, or payments.

02 / CHECKUse a known route

Open the official app or call a verified number you already have.

03 / REPORTAlert the right team

Use your organization's report button or contact IT/security directly.

04 / RECOVERAct quickly if needed

If you clicked or shared data, tell IT or your bank promptly; change reused passwords from the genuine site.

Everyday security habits

Make accounts harder to take over

Use a unique password for every account and store it in a reputable password manager. Turn on MFA, preferably passkeys or a hardware security key where available. Never share one-time codes or approve a sign-in you did not start.

Make reporting routine

Use your workplace's reporting path, keep software and browsers updated, and tell IT or your bank promptly if you clicked, downloaded, paid, or shared credentials. Reporting quickly is more useful than feeling embarrassed.
